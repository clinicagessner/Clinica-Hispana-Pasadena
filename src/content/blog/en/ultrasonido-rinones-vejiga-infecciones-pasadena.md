Rosa has stopped counting. This is the third time this year the burning has come back: she gets checked, takes her antibiotic, feels better, and a few weeks later it starts all over again. Martín has never had burning at all, but one side of his back has been aching for days, and he can't tell whether it's from lifting boxes at the warehouse or whether "it's the kidney," as a friend insisted. Two very different stories that often land on the same question at the clinic: is it worth taking a look at the kidneys and bladder? Here is when the clinic's medical team considers an ultrasound for that, what the images can reveal and, just as important, what they can't answer.

## First things first: is it your back or your kidney?

People call almost any ache around the waist "kidney pain," and most of the time it's a muscle. The kidneys sit higher and farther back than most of us imagine, tucked under the lowest ribs, one on each side. These clues help tell the two apart:

| Clue | Usually a muscle | Points toward the kidney |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it hurts | Across the low back, near the belt line | On the side, under the ribs, sometimes spreading to the groin |
| What changes it | Worse when you bend or twist, better at rest | Barely changes with position; may come in waves |
| How it started | After lifting, a bad night's sleep or a sudden move | With no strain beforehand, sometimes all at once |
| What comes with it | Stiffness, a sore spot when pressed | Fever, nausea, bloody urine, burning when you pee |

The table is no substitute for an exam. What it does is organize what you'll tell the medical team: if your pain sounds more like the right-hand column, say it in exactly those words.

## When a look at the kidneys and bladder comes up

Not every urinary infection calls for imaging. An occasional bladder infection that clears up nicely with antibiotics is normally handled with a urine test and treatment. Ultrasound joins the conversation in situations like these:

- **Infections that keep returning:** three or more in a year, or two close together. The clinic's [urinary tract infection](/en/services/infecciones-urinarias) page puts it plainly: repeat infections deserve a workup with a culture, glucose and, in some cases, a urinary tract ultrasound.
- **An infection that doesn't improve** as expected, or flares up again right after the antibiotic ends.
- **Side pain together with blood in the urine,** which raises the question of a stone.
- **A man with a urinary infection,** which is less common and usually warrants a closer look.
- **A weak stream or the feeling that your bladder never fully empties,** especially in men over 50.

During the visit, the medical team puts together your history, the physical exam and the urine result, then decides with you whether an ultrasound adds anything in your case.

## What the images can reveal

On the clinic's [ultrasound](/en/services/ultrasonido) page, the kidneys are part of the abdominal scan, and the bladder (plus the prostate in men) is part of the pelvic scan. Generally speaking, those images are used to look for:

- **Stones:** those sitting inside the kidney often show up as bright spots that cast a shadow behind them.
- **A swollen kidney:** when urine can't drain properly, the kidney looks "puffed up," a hint that something is blocking the way, such as a stone stuck farther down.
- **Urine left behind in the bladder:** in this kind of scan you're sometimes asked to empty your bladder and then it's checked again to see how much remained. A bladder that doesn't empty well gives bacteria an easy way back.
- **The bladder wall** and its shape.
- **An enlarged prostate,** as general information: its size can be seen on the screen, but that alone doesn't explain the cause, so it's completed with the [men's health](/en/services/salud-hombre) evaluation.

## What ultrasound can't tell you

This is where most of the confusion lives. A normal scan doesn't rule out an infection, because **ultrasound can't see bacteria**. An infection is confirmed with the urine test, and finding out which germ it is and which antibiotic works against it takes a urine culture, whose result needs a few days. The three pieces fit together; none of them replaces the others.

It isn't flawless either: a very small stone, especially one traveling down the tube between kidney and bladder, may be hard to spot. If what turns up calls for a different kind of study or a specialized evaluation, the medical team explains why and guides the referral.

## Showing up ready

Preparation depends on what's being checked, so confirm it when you call:

1. **A full bladder:** to see the bladder, drink a few glasses of water an hour before and hold it until the scan is over. Arriving with an empty bladder is the top reason people end up waiting.
2. **Fasting, if your abdomen is being scanned too:** for the abdominal scan the clinic asks for 6 to 8 hours without food.
3. **Two-piece clothing,** so only your belly and back need to be uncovered.
4. **Your urinary history on paper:** rough dates of each infection, which antibiotic you took and whether a culture was ever done. If frequent bathroom trips are part of the picture, the three-day log described in [urinating all the time](/en/blog/orinar-a-cada-rato-causas-pasadena) is useful too.
5. **Earlier results,** if another clinic ever ran tests on you.

## What the scan feels like

You lie on your back, warm gel goes on your skin and a small handheld probe glides over your belly. For the kidneys you may be asked to roll onto your side or take a deep breath and hold it for a few seconds, which pushes them down into better view. There's no radiation and no pain; the full bladder tends to be the least pleasant part. According to the clinic, the scan takes 15 to 30 minutes, and the medical team reviews the images with you during the visit, explaining what they see in Spanish or English.

Back to Rosa: for her, an ultrasound doesn't take the place of a culture, but it can show whether her bladder empties properly or whether a stone is giving bacteria somewhere to hide. And for Martín, if the pain turns out to be muscular, he can stop worrying about his kidney; if it isn't, the scan helps decide what comes next.

## Don't wait for an ultrasound if this happens

Some moments can't wait for a scan. If that side pain arrives with a high fever and shaking chills, the infection may have climbed to the kidney, and that belongs in the emergency room, not in an ultrasound visit. The same goes for vomiting so persistent you can't keep down water or your pill, a bladder that feels ready to burst while nothing comes out, urine that turns deep red or shows clots, or pain so intense that no position helps or that makes you faint. In any of those cases, head to the nearest ER or dial 911.
