Your belt usually knows before any lab test does. First you move it one notch, then another, and one day your usual jeans won't button even though the scale has barely moved. Plenty of people chalk it up to age or a desk job. Yet that growing belly is one of five pieces of something health teams call metabolic syndrome, and when several of those pieces show up in the same person, the heart and arteries end up working harder than anyone can feel.

This post does not walk through each lab result on its own. The point is to look at them together, like a puzzle, because that is how they add up to the risk of a heart attack or a stroke.

## Five numbers that are read together

Metabolic syndrome is not a disease with one telltale symptom. It is a name for a group of risk factors that tend to travel together. According to the [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI)](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/metabolic-syndrome/diagnosis), a person has metabolic syndrome when at least three of these five are present:

| Piece | Level that counts as a risk factor |
| --- | --- |
| Waist | Over 40 inches in men, over 35 in women |
| Blood pressure | 130/85 or higher on repeated readings |
| Fasting glucose | 100 mg/dL or higher |
| Triglycerides | 150 mg/dL or higher |
| HDL cholesterol | Under 40 mg/dL in men, under 50 in women |

If you already take treatment for one of them, say for blood pressure, that piece still counts even when today's reading looks fine.

What catches people off guard is that none of the five has to be sky high. Someone with a blood pressure of 132/86, a fasting glucose of 104 and a 41-inch waist may hear "you're running a little high" at every visit without anyone pointing out that, taken together, that picture already meets the definition.

## Why it matters where the fat sits

Not all body fat behaves the same way. Fat stored on the hips and thighs is fairly quiet. Fat that settles deep inside the abdomen, around the liver and intestines, is far more active: it releases substances that push triglycerides up, make insulin work less well and feed inflammation in artery walls. That is why the NHLBI notes in its [healthy weight guide](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart-healthy-living/healthy-weight) that carrying most of your fat around the waist rather than the hips raises the risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.

This leads to something many people find surprising: a person with a "normal" number on the scale but a round belly can have more puzzle pieces than a heavier person whose weight is spread out. Total weight matters, but the tape measure picks up things the scale cannot.

## How to measure your waist at home

All you need is a soft sewing tape that does not stretch. The NHLBI describes the method below, and following it closely is what makes the number useful:

1. **Stand up straight,** barefoot, with nothing bulky around your middle; bare skin works best.
2. **Feel for your hip bones,** the top edge on each side.
3. **Wrap the tape around your abdomen** just above those bones. Not at the belly button and not where your pants button, which usually sits lower.
4. **Keep the tape level,** parallel to the floor in front and in back. A mirror helps, or ask someone at home to check your back.
5. **Make it snug, not tight:** the tape should rest flat without pressing into the skin.
6. **Breathe out normally** and read the number at the end of the breath. No sucking in.

Measure twice, write down the average along with the date, and repeat it once a month, at the same time of day and before breakfast. A trend tells you far more than a single snapshot.

## What the mirror cannot show

Of the five pieces, only the waist is easy to check at home. Blood pressure can be tracked with an upper-arm cuff, but the other three live in your blood and cause no discomfort. Nobody feels triglycerides at 190 or a fasting glucose of 106. That is why so many people find out about metabolic syndrome by accident: on a work physical, at a checkup before a trip, or once diabetes has already shown up.

There are a few clues that make an earlier check worthwhile: loud snoring with daytime tiredness, dark velvety patches on the neck or armpits, diabetes during a past pregnancy, or a parent with diabetes or high blood pressure.

## What gets checked at the clinic

With your waist size written down, a visit at our Spencer Hwy office fills in the rest of the picture. As part of [diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol care](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas), the clinic's medical team reviews your history and medications and takes your blood pressure, weight and glucose. From there they may order:

- **Blood work** with fasting glucose, A1C and a lipid panel, which is where the triglyceride and HDL numbers come from. Samples are drawn under [blood tests](/en/services/examenes-sangre); for fasting glucose and lipids, 8 to 12 hours without food is recommended, and water is fine.
- **Kidney and liver function,** since belly fat and fatty liver often go hand in hand.
- **An [EKG](/en/services/electrocardiograma)** when the medical team sees fit, for example after years of high blood pressure or if you have never had one.

For men, blood pressure, weight and lab checks are also part of [men's health](/en/services/salud-hombre), so if you come in for something else it is worth asking to add them.

Once all the numbers are side by side, it becomes clear how many pieces you have and which ones weigh most in your case. If treatment is needed for blood pressure, glucose or lipids, the prescription is sent electronically to the pharmacy of your choice. And if you already take something, don't stop or switch it on your own even when your numbers improve: talk with the medical team before changing anything.

## The good news: the pieces move together

Just as the five pieces tend to arrive as a group, they also tend to improve as a group. The NHLBI explains that losing 3% to 5% of your current weight can already lower triglycerides and glucose, and that losing a bit more helps blood pressure and HDL too. For someone at 200 pounds, that means starting with roughly 6 to 10 pounds, not a magazine makeover.

A few changes that often show up on the tape measure:

- **Swap the drinks before the food.** Soda, sweetened aguas frescas, juice and sugary coffee add up fast without filling you up.
- **Move most days.** A brisk walk after dinner, even 15 or 20 minutes, helps that evening's blood sugar.
- **Cut back on alcohol,** which raises triglycerides more than most people expect.
- **Sleep better.** Short nights and snoring with breathing pauses are linked to bigger appetite and poorer sugar control.
- **Track progress with the tape,** not only the scale: sometimes the waist shrinks before the weight does.

## Warning signs that can't wait for a visit

If you ever have severe chest pain or pressure, sudden shortness of breath, one side of the face drooping, weakness in one arm or slurred speech, call 911. Skip the clinic and leave the car keys alone: paramedics begin treatment before you even reach the ER.
