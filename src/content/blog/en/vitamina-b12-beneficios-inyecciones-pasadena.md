"Does the vitamin B12 shot hurt?" is one of the most frequent questions that reach the clinic, along with "how often is it given?" and "does it really fix tiredness?". This practical guide answers those doubts with what happens at the visit, without miracle promises.

## Does the B12 shot hurt?

A little, and briefly. It is given into the shoulder or buttock muscle with a fine needle; you feel a prick and sometimes a short sting as the liquid goes in. The area may be a bit tender that day. No rest is needed: you walk out and go on with your routine.

## What is vitamin B12 for?

It makes red blood cells and keeps the nerves healthy. When it is lacking, you get tiredness that sleep does not fix, tingling in hands or feet, trouble concentrating, paleness and, in advanced cases, anemia. The injection replaces the vitamin directly into the blood, without relying on the stomach to absorb it.

## Who really needs it?

Anyone with low B12 on a blood test, or with factors that lower it: a vegetarian or vegan diet, age over 50, long-term use of metformin or acid-reducing medications, stomach or bariatric surgery, and bowel diseases. If you have no deficiency, the shot does not give "extra energy": the body clears the excess. That is why it is worth measuring first.

## Shot or pill?

If the deficiency is mild and the gut absorbs well, a daily pill works. If the deficiency is marked, there are neurological symptoms, or the problem is absorption (metformin, antacids, surgery, age), the injection is safer because it bypasses the stomach. The medical team decides with your result in hand.

## How often is it given?

It depends on the starting level. A usual schedule for deficiency is one shot a week for the first weeks and then one a month for maintenance; with neurological symptoms it may start more often. Without a confirmed deficiency, routine shots make no sense. The blood level is rechecked after a few months to adjust.
