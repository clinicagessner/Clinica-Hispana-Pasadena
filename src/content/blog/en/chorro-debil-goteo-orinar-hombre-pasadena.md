You stand at the toilet and nothing comes for a while. When it does, the flow is lazy, stops halfway, and once you have zipped up, a few drops show up on your underwear. Sometimes it does, but not always, and nearly every cause has something that can be done about it. Below we go through where each complaint comes from, which changes are expected over the years and which ones call for a check.

## Put a name to what you notice

Not every urinary complaint means the same thing. These five tend to travel together because they involve getting urine out, not the urge to go:

- **Slow to start:** several seconds pass, or you have to strain, before anything flows.
- **Weak stream:** it comes out with little force or sprays, and takes longer than it used to.
- **Stop-and-start stream:** the flow halts and restarts once or more during the same trip.
- **Dribbling at the end:** drops leak out on their own seconds or minutes later, often while walking.
- **Feeling that urine is left:** you leave the bathroom and soon feel you are not finished.

Spend a few days noting which of the five you have and whether it is getting worse: you will be asked first thing.

## What is usually behind it?

### A prostate that grows with age

The prostate wraps around the tube that carries urine out, just below the bladder. With age it tends to grow in a benign way, very common after 50, and as it grows it squeezes that tube like a foot on a garden hose. The usual result is a hard start, a soft stream and incomplete emptying. Growth does not mean cancer, but only an evaluation sorts that out.

### A narrowed urethra

A section of the urethra can tighten because of scar tissue left by an injury to the area, an old infection or a catheter used during a hospital stay. The stream then tends to be very thin or to veer off, and it can happen to young men too.

### The pelvic floor muscles

Beneath the bladder are muscles that support it and help squeeze out the last of the urine. When they are weak, the urethra holds on to a little liquid that seeps out later: the most frequent reason for the drops that appear after you zip up. When they are tight from stress or pain, the opposite happens and relaxing to start becomes hard.

### Infection or inflammation

A urinary infection or an inflamed prostate can change the stream from one day to the next, along with burning, sudden urges, cloudy urine or discomfort in the lower belly. In men it should be confirmed with testing, not treated by guesswork.

### Constipation

A full bowel pushes against the bladder and the neck where urine exits. Straining hard on the toilet also wears out the pelvic floor.

### Some medicines and diabetes

Certain cold, allergy, mood or pain remedies can make the bladder squeeze less forcefully or tighten its outlet; if the symptoms began when you started a new medicine, say so. Long-standing diabetes can damage the nerves that signal and move the bladder, and high sugar also makes you urinate more.

## What changes with age and what is no longer normal

| Expected over the years | Worth a check |
|---|---|
| The stream is a bit weaker than at 30 | You have to strain or push with your belly to urinate |
| One or two drops at the end, now and then | Daily dribbling that wets clothing, or leaks you cannot control |
| A few extra seconds to get started | The stream stops almost every time or worsens within weeks |
| Getting up once at night | The bladder still feels full after going, or urinating hurts |
| Slow changes over many years | Pink, red or persistently foul-smelling urine |

The rule of thumb: if something changed quickly, gets in the way of your work or your sleep, or comes with pain, burning or blood, it is not just age.

## Simple habits that often help

These steps are safe for almost any man and do not replace an evaluation, but they often ease the dribbling and the unfinished feeling:

1. **Go in two rounds.** When you think you are done, wait 20 or 30 seconds, relax and try again. That second round empties what was left behind.
2. **Milk the urethra at the end.** With two fingers, press gently just behind the scrotum and slide forward along the underside of the penis to push out the drops still sitting in the tube before you get dressed.
3. **Do not hold it in.** Holding for hours stretches the bladder, and afterward it struggles to contract. Go when you feel the signal, especially on long work shifts.
4. **Strengthen the pelvic floor.** Tighten the muscles you would use to stop the flow, hold a few seconds and let go; repeat several times a day, but never while actually urinating.
5. **Spread out your fluids.** Drink through the day and cut back a couple of hours before bed; go easy on alcohol and caffeine, which irritate the bladder.
6. **Look after your bowels.** Fiber, water and daily movement to keep constipation away.

If after a few weeks of these changes nothing improves, or it gets worse, it is time for a visit.

## Signs that mean the ER today

Any of the following means dialing 911 or heading to the nearest ER right now, not booking a visit:

- Hours have gone by without a single drop coming out and your lower belly is hard, swollen and sore.
- You run a high fever with shaking and it hurts to urinate, in your lower back or between your legs.
- Urine comes out with thick blood or clots, or stops because a clot is blocking it.
- Severe pain in your side that moves toward the groin, will not let you sit still and comes with vomiting.
