A lot of people ride out the first days of the flu at home with tea, fever reducers and the hope that it clears up by itself. For most healthy adults, that works. But one detail changes the math: prescription antiviral medicines for influenza have a short window when they help the most, and that window tends to close while you are still deciding whether a clinic visit is "worth it." Here is what these medicines do, why timing matters and who should not wait.

## What a flu antiviral actually does

A flu antiviral does not wipe out the virus overnight. It slows the virus down so it stops copying itself inside your body. According to the CDC, when used appropriately it can make symptoms milder, shorten the time you are sick a little and lower the risk of complications such as pneumonia, especially for people at higher risk.

It is a prescription medicine, given after an evaluation. It is not something you buy on your own, keep from one season to the next or hand to a relative who "has the same thing." And it only works against influenza: it does nothing for a common cold or a bacterial infection, which is a different problem with a different treatment.

## Why timing matters: the first two days

The flu virus multiplies fastest early in the illness. Hence the CDC's advice: these medicines pay off most when they are started within the first two days of symptoms, while the virus is still gaining ground. The further along the infection is, the less room the medicine has to slow the virus.

That does not mean nothing can be done after day two. For people at high risk, or when the flu is turning into something more serious, the clinic's medical team may still consider treatment later on. But if you or someone at home belongs to a higher-risk group, the practical move is not to spend those first days waiting to see how it goes.

## How to tell which flu day you are on

It sounds simple, yet it is one of the hardest questions for patients to answer at the visit. It helps to have this clear:

1. **Write down when it started.** Not when you felt worst, but the very first symptom: chills, body aches, fever or a scratchy throat.
2. **Notice whether it hit suddenly.** Influenza tends to start all at once, with fever and heavy fatigue; a cold creeps in gradually.
3. **Log your temperature and the time** whenever you check it, plus any fever reducer you took.
4. **Think about whether someone at home or work had the flu** in the days before; it is useful for the evaluation.

With that information, the clinic's medical team can tell more precisely where you are in the illness.

## Who should not wait to get checked

The CDC advises taking the flu seriously, and considering treatment early, for people with a higher risk of complications:

- Adults 65 and older.
- Pregnant women and women in the two weeks after giving birth.
- Young children, especially those under 2.
- People with asthma, COPD or another lung condition.
- People with diabetes, heart disease or kidney disease.
- People with a weakened immune system from illness or medication.
- People with severe obesity and residents of nursing homes or long-term care.

If you have diabetes or high blood pressure and already come to us for [chronic condition care](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas), a flu is a reason to come in early, not to wait for your next routine follow-up.

## What makes sense in your situation

| Your situation | What makes sense |
| --- | --- |
| Fever and body aches came on suddenly yesterday, and you are in a higher-risk group | Get checked today, before it gets worse |
| Strong symptoms for one or two days, no other health conditions | Get checked soon to confirm whether it is influenza and weigh the options |
| Mild symptoms for almost a week and you are improving | Rest, fluids, and a visit if the fever comes back or you get worse |
| Short of breath, chest pain or confusion | Call 911 or go to the emergency room |

## What happens at the visit

At the clinic, a rapid influenza A and B test, plus a COVID-19 test when needed, is done with a nasal swab; the result comes back in minutes while you are still there. Then the medical team checks your throat, ears and chest, asks when the symptoms began and what conditions or medications you have, and uses all of that to decide whether an antiviral makes sense for you or whether treating the symptoms at home is enough.

A few things worth knowing:

- **A negative test does not always rule out the flu.** If the symptoms fit and you are at high risk, the decision does not rest on the result alone.
- **If your throat is the main complaint,** with white patches and no cough, the medical team may add a [strep test](/en/services/prueba-strep) before you leave.
- **If the result is COVID-19,** there are also antivirals indicated in the first days for people at higher risk; the same don't-wait logic applies.
- **If an antiviral is recommended,** ask how to take it, what to do if it upsets your stomach and when you should come back.

## What an antiviral does not replace

An antiviral is a tool for when the flu has already arrived. It does not replace the [flu vaccine](/en/services/vacunas), given every season and still the best way to lower your odds of a severe illness. Nor does it replace the basics: rest, plenty of fluids, staying home while you have a fever and going back to your routine once you have gone a full day fever-free without medicine to bring it down.

While you are sick, wear a mask if you live with a baby or an older adult, wash your hands often and keep the rooms aired out.

## Signs this is not the time to wait for a clinic

Antiviral or not, some signs mean calling 911 or heading to the emergency room: trouble breathing or shortness of breath at rest, chest pain or pressure, bluish lips or nails, confusion or unusual drowsiness, seizures, or a fever that improves and then comes back stronger. In a child, also breathing very fast, refusing to drink or being very listless.

## What to bring if you come in with flu symptoms

- The approximate date and time your symptoms started, plus your temperature notes.
- Your medication list or bottles, including over-the-counter products.
- A possible pregnancy matters for the evaluation and for any medicine you are given, so bring it up at check-in.
- A mask, or ask for one at the front desk to protect others in the waiting area.
