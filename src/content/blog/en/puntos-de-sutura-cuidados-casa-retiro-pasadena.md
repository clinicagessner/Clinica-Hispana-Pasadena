You left the visit with your hand wrapped, a folded instruction sheet in your pocket and your mind still on the scare of the cut. In the car, heading back along Spencer Highway, the questions start: can I get it wet tonight? Should I put rubbing alcohol on it? What if a stitch pops open while I sleep? Who takes them out, and when?

Closing the wound is half the job. The other half happens at home, while the skin knits itself back together beneath the threads, and that's what this guide covers.

## First: hold on to your instructions

Every wound is different. A cut finger on someone who works construction isn't cared for the same way as a small incision on the back after a cyst was removed. That's why [wound suturing](/en/services/suturas-heridas) always comes with after-care instructions for your case: when you can wet the area, how often to change the dressing and which date to come back.

If the sheet got lost or something wasn't clear, call the clinic before you improvise.

## Clean and dry: the rule that matters most

During the first days, the dressing put on at the clinic shields the wound from dust, sweat and rubbing. Leave it in place and keep it dry for as long as you were told. Once the team says you can wash it:

1. Wash your hands well with soap and water before touching the area.
2. Peel the dressing off gently; if it's stuck, dampen it a little so it lets go on its own.
3. Wash the wound with water and a mild soap, without scrubbing the stitches.
4. Pat dry with a clean towel or gauze. No rubbing.
5. Cover it again with a fresh dressing if those were your instructions.

MedlinePlus, the health information site of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, advises against putting rubbing alcohol, hydrogen peroxide or iodine on a stitched wound unless you were told to: they can irritate the new skin and slow healing. Water and mild soap are almost always enough.

## Can I shower with stitches?

Yes, in most cases, with two conditions. One: wait until you're cleared to get the wound wet. Two: showers yes, soaking no.

Under the shower, let soapy water run over the stitches without aiming a hard spray straight at them, and dry them as soon as you step out. What you need to avoid is submerging the wound. The clinic's suturing page puts it plainly: do not soak the wound in a pool or bathtub until the stitches are out. The same goes for a hot tub, a lake or the beach at Galveston: standing water or water with bacteria is an open door for infection.

## The dressing: when to change it

Change the dressing when you were told to and, on top of that, every time it gets wet, dirty or soaked with blood. A little clear or pinkish fluid on the gauze early on is normal; blood that soaks through the dressing again and again is not.

If you don't feel confident changing it, or the wound sits where you can't see it well, the dressing change can be done at the clinic. Wounds that were stitched and need a dressing change or stitch removal are handled under [wound care](/en/services/curacion-heridas), walk-in and in Spanish or English.

## What not to do while you have stitches

- **Don't pull or snip the threads**, even if they itch or look long. A stitch taken out too early can leave the wound gaping.
- **Don't pick at the scab.** It's part of the closing process; tearing it off widens the scar.
- **Skip home remedies**: no aloe straight from the yard, no ointments nobody prescribed, no powders, no coffee grounds.
- **Don't strain the area.** If the stitches are over a joint, on your hand or near a muscle you use for lifting, avoid stretching it or heavy effort until they come out.
- **Keep it out of the sun.** Freshly closed skin discolors easily.
- **Don't submerge the wound**, as covered above, until removal.

## Signs that something is off

It's normal for the edge to look a little red, slightly swollen and sore during the first days, and for that discomfort to ease bit by bit. What isn't normal is for it to get worse. The clinic's suturing and wound care pages list these signs of infection:

- Redness that grows and spreads beyond the edge of the wound.
- Warmth in the area.
- Swelling that increases.
- Pus or a bad smell.
- Pain that climbs instead of easing.
- Red streaks running up the skin.
- Fever.

With any of them, don't wait for your removal date: come in sooner. Should the visit confirm that the stitched cut is infected, the care team at Clínica Hispana Nueva Salud Pasadena looks at the wound, decides whether an antibiotic is needed and, if it is, sends the prescription electronically to whichever pharmacy you pick, so you can start it on your way home. When pus collects under the skin, it may need [drainage](/en/services/drenaje-abscesos).

**Call 911 or go to the ER** if the wound won't stop bleeding despite pressure, if you see red streaks climbing quickly, if red skin spreads fast and you also have a fever, or if the person is confused or very weak.

## When do stitches come out?

It depends on the part of the body, because skin in each area heals at its own pace. The clinic's [wound suturing](/en/services/suturas-heridas) page gives these general references:

| Body area | General reference for removal |
| --- | --- |
| Face | 5 to 7 days |
| Trunk and scalp | Between 7 and 10 days |
| Arms and legs | 10 to 14 days |
| Joints | Up to 14 days |

These are references, not a fixed appointment: the exact date for your case is the one you were given when the wound was closed. Removal is done at the clinic in minutes, with no appointment. Don't try to take them out yourself with nail scissors: it's easy to nick the skin or leave a piece of thread behind.

There's one exception: adhesives and some absorbable stitches fall off on their own. If you were told yours are that kind, there's no need to come back for removal, though it's still worth returning if you spot any of the warning signs.

If at the visit you don't remember your last tetanus shot, it is reviewed that day; vaccine information is on the [vaccines](/en/services/vacunas) page.

## After removal: look after the scar

No more threads doesn't mean the skin is as strong as before. A scar keeps maturing for months: early on it looks pink or purplish and, over time, it lightens and flattens.

What helps most, according to the clinic's [minor surgery](/en/services/cirugias-menores) page, is keeping the scar out of the sun for months. Wear clothing that covers it, or sunscreen once it's fully closed, especially if you work outdoors in Pasadena or around South Houston, where the sun hits hard nearly all year.

Other general tips:

- Keep washing with water and mild soap, and keep the skin moisturized.
- Avoid bumps and heavy rubbing on the area for the first few weeks.
- If the scar turns thick, raised, very red or keeps itching, bring it up at your next visit.

Every stitched wound leaves some mark; home care helps it end up thinner.

## A checklist for the fridge door

- Dressing dry and in place until you're cleared to wash.
- Water and mild soap, pat dry.
- Showers yes; tub, pool and beach no, until removal.
- No rubbing alcohol, peroxide or home remedies.
- Watch for spreading redness, pus, warmth, rising pain or fever.
