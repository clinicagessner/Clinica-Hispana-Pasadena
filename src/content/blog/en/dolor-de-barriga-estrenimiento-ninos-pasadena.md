It's seven in the morning, the backpack is by the door, and your six-year-old is curled up on the couch: "my tummy hurts." Yesterday it was the same thing right before school, and on Sunday too, after the cookout at her grandparents' place. No fever, she picks at breakfast, plays for a while and forgets about it. Is this worth worrying about, or is it just "a kid thing"?

The short answer: most childhood tummy aches are not serious, and one of the most frequent culprits is something as unglamorous as constipation. Still, a handful of signs change the picture completely, and it pays to know them before they ever show up.

## The suspect almost nobody checks: poop

Many families ask about gas, something bad they ate or parasites, and very few ask how many times their child went to the bathroom that week. According to the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), constipation is very common in children and a frequent reason for belly pain, and it often goes unnoticed because the child does poop, just not much, or it comes out hard and painful.

Clues that constipation may be behind it:

- Fewer than two bowel movements a week, which is the yardstick the NIDDK uses, or stools that come out as hard pellets or very wide.
- It hurts to push, they cry, or they hide behind furniture and stand on tiptoe: that is usually holding it in, not pushing.
- Poop stains show up in the underwear even though the child has been out of diapers for a long time; sometimes that is soft stool leaking around a hard mass.
- There are streaks of bright red blood on the toilet paper from a small tear.
- The pain sits around the belly button, comes and goes, and eases after a big bowel movement.

## Why kids get constipated

There is rarely a single cause. The usual story is a loop: a hard stool hurts, the child holds the next one to avoid the pain, the bowel pulls more water out of what stays inside, and the next one comes out even harder.

The most common nudges into that loop:

- **Changes in routine.** Starting kindergarten, a holiday trip to Mexico, moving, or spending the weekend at another house.
- **The school bathroom.** Plenty of elementary students would rather wait all day than use a stall without a door or squeeze it into a short recess.
- **Potty training** that is pushed before the child is ready.
- **Few fruits and vegetables, little water,** and lots of fast food, white bread or cheese.
- **Too much milk** in kids who drink several cups a day and barely eat anything else.
- **Screens and little movement,** which slow the gut down.

## Other causes of belly pain worth knowing

Not everything is constipation. This table sums up general clues; it is not a way to diagnose at home, but it helps you describe what is going on at the visit.

| Possible cause | Clue that often comes with it |
| --- | --- |
| Constipation | Hard stools or few per week; relief after going |
| Strep throat | Fever, pain when swallowing, sometimes stomachache and nausea, no cough or runny nose |
| Urinary infection | Burning when peeing, going often, wetting the bed again, pain below the belly button |
| Gut infection or parasites | Diarrhea that won't quit, repeated cramps, itchy bottom at night |
| Nerves or stress | Pain on school days or before a test; gone on the weekend |
| Something that didn't agree | Starts after a meal and fades on its own fairly soon |

Two details many parents don't expect. First, strep in the throat can show up as a tummy ache, especially in grade-school kids, and sometimes the throat barely complains. Second, constipation and bladder trouble often travel together, because a full bowel presses on the bladder. A child who starts wetting the bed again or keeps getting urinary infections also deserves a question about their poop.

## What you can try at home this week

If none of the warning signs listed further down are present, these changes help many children with mild constipation:

1. **Water first.** A personal bottle in the backpack that has to come home empty works better than telling them to "drink more water."
2. **Real fiber on the plate.** Beans, oatmeal, corn, pears, prunes, papaya, apples with the skin on, and corn tortillas instead of white bread.
3. **A set time on the toilet.** Five to ten minutes after breakfast or dinner, unhurried and without a screen, taking advantage of the reflex a meal wakes up.
4. **A small stool under the feet.** With the knees higher than the hips, pushing takes less effort; on an adult toilet a child's feet just dangle.
5. **No scolding.** If they soil their underwear, it is almost never on purpose. Punishment only makes them want to hold it in more.

Don't give laxatives, suppositories, enemas or "stomach teas" on your own. Some home remedies irritate the bowel or mask what is really happening, and what worked for a cousin won't always work for your child.

## Before the visit: a tummy diary

The question "how long has it hurt?" almost always gets a "well… a while." A few days of notes beat any memory. Jot down on your phone:

- Every bowel movement and what it looked like: pellets, a cracked sausage, soft or liquid.
- What time the pain shows up, where they point and how long it lasts.
- What they ate and how much water they drank that day.
- Whether there was fever, vomiting, burning when peeing or a wet bed at night.
- Whether the pain comes more on school days than on weekends.

## Warning signs: ER or 911

Don't wait for the diary or the visit if your child has:

- **Severe pain in the lower right side** of the belly that gets worse when walking, jumping or coughing.
- **Green or bloody vomit,** or vomiting that won't stop.
- **A hard, swollen belly** that hurts a lot when touched.
- **Blood in the stool** beyond a small streak on the paper, or black stool.
- **High fever with listlessness:** very sleepy, not responding as usual or not wanting to move.
- In boys, pain or swelling in a testicle along with the belly pain.

With any of those signs, this is no longer a step-stool-and-diary situation: dial 911 or take your child straight to the nearest emergency room.
